### History and future editorial note

Supply chains existed long before 1970. One verified milestone in this pack is the first retail UPC barcode scan on 26 June 1974 [1]. It is an identification and automation example, not the birth of supply chain management.

Other “picture 1970” scenes are illustrative teaching devices, not universal historical descriptions. Present-day scenes are exercises, and all 2050 scenes are imagined possibilities, not forecasts. Earlier practitioners also understood coordination, uncertainty and responsible use of resources.

The shared case and all quantities, lead times, requirements and test records are fictional.

## Welcome post — Your Supply Chain Origin Story begins here

🌱 **THE ORIGIN STORY: SUPPLY CHAIN MANAGEMENT**  
**A free 7-day challenge from SuccessLabs Academy**

A notebook reaches a learner. A parcel reaches a customer. A spare part reaches a workshop.

Behind each arrival are people, materials, information and decisions.

Over seven days, spend **10–15 minutes a day** exploring how those connections work—and what happens when one of them breaks.

**Supply Chain Management, or SCM, coordinates the activities and partners that help meet demand, from sourcing and preparation through delivery and returns.**

Procurement helps decide how to obtain what is needed. Logistics helps move and store it. SCM connects these with planning, inventory, operations and other activities.

## Day 1 — See the Network Behind the Item

🎯 **DAY 1 OF 7 — THE SUPPLY CHAIN MINDSET SHIFT**

Welcome! Today, look at an everyday item and imagine the connections behind it.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes: read, reflect and share.**

### 💭 Twenty learners arrive. Where are their kits?

The supplier says the order was dispatched.

But the cartons went to another building. Nobody knows who should receive them.

Buying happened. Transport happened. The learners still cannot begin.

**SCM connects the steps so that demand can be met in the intended place, time, quantity and condition.**

### 🕰️ A brief journey: 1970 → 2050

**1970s — A real milestone:** On 26 June 1974, a pack of chewing gum with a UPC barcode was scanned at a supermarket in Troy, Ohio [1]. It is one example of shared identification helping connect products with information.

**Today:** Ask whether the plan, stock record, delivery message and physical goods tell a consistent story.

**Imagine 2050:** AI could help coordinate supply options and spot disruptions earlier. People would still need to check data, set priorities and remain accountable for trade-offs.

The future scene is a possibility, not a prediction.

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“My task is finished.” → “Has the need been met across the whole flow?”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Late materials affect learners, organisers and delivery staff. Excess materials can create waste.

Think about who depends on the flow and who experiences its failures.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**A supply chain can fail even when each team reports success.**

A successful local task is not always a successful end-to-end outcome.

### ✍️ Your 5-minute action

- **My item or order:** ___
- **Who needs it and why:** ___
- **How much, where and by when:** ___
- **One condition that matters:** ___
- **One connection that could fail:** ___

**Example:** Twenty complete learning kits at the community room before Friday's 10 a.m. workshop; pens must work; the receiver may not know the delivery is coming.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Keep your need sentence. It is your **Origin Story**. You can label it **NEED-01**, but the label is optional.

🎉 **Today's win:** You can connect an item with a human need and an end-to-end result.

## Day 2 — Map the Flow and the Handoffs

🔍 **DAY 2 OF 7 — FOLLOW MATERIALS AND INFORMATION**

Yesterday, you chose a supply need. Today, follow how it could be met.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. No diagramming tool required.**

### 💭 The cartons arrive, but nobody accepts them

The delivery driver has a location. The organiser has an order. The venue team has no receiving instructions.

What is missing?

The goods need a route. So does the information.

A **handoff** is where responsibility, goods or information pass between people or teams.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** A delivery note travels with a carton.

**Today:** A delivery message may arrive separately from the goods. People still need to connect the two.

**Imagine 2050:** Systems could share more updates automatically. People still check whether the right owner received accurate information.

The question: **“Who needs to know what before the next step?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“Move the item.” → “Move the item with the information needed to use it.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

A missed handoff can cause another trip, unnecessary waiting or rushed work.

Clear receiving instructions respect the time of staff and partners.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**Sometimes the missing item is a message.**

A named receiver and a clear location may solve more than an extra tracking screen.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Map three stages for your chosen example.

For the kits, try:

1. Confirm need and arrange supply.
2. Receive, count and check.
3. Stage, distribute and collect reusable leftovers.

Reply:

- **My supply need:** ___
- **Stage 1:** ___; owner ___; information needed ___.
- **Stage 2:** ___; owner ___; information needed ___.
- **Stage 3:** ___; owner ___; information needed ___.
- **One handoff I would clarify:** ___

A role such as “organiser” is enough; no real person needs to be named.

Remember that an actual supply chain may be a network with several suppliers and routes. This is a small starting map.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Which handoff most affects your Day 1 need? Keep that connection. Note changes if your understanding evolves.

🎉 **Today's win:** You have a small flow map with ownership and information.

## Day 3 — How Much Is Enough?

📦 **DAY 3 OF 7 — CONNECT DEMAND, STOCK AND SUPPLY**

You have a flow map. Today, check the quantities behind it.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Simple subtraction is enough.**

### 💭 Do we really need to order twenty kits?

For this fictional exercise:

- Twenty learners each need one complete kit.
- Six complete kits are already available, usable and not reserved for anything else.
- Fourteen additional complete kits are confirmed to arrive before the receiving and preparation deadline.
- There is no other demand, loss or quantity constraint in this simple calculation.

| Quantity view | Complete kits |
|---|---:|
| Demand for the workshop | 20 |
| Usable, unallocated stock already available | 6 |
| Additional quantity needed before considering incoming supply | 14 |
| Confirmed incoming supply due in time | 14 |
| **Remaining projected gap** | **0** |

**Demand** is what is needed. **Inventory** is stock held. Incoming supply is expected—not physically received yet.

A projected gap of zero depends on those assumptions holding.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** Someone counts stock before placing an order.

**Today:** Compare physical availability, reservations and expected receipts.

**Imagine 2050:** AI suggests replenishment quantities. People still check demand assumptions, item condition and arrival timing.

The question: **“What is actually available for this need?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“We need twenty, so buy twenty.” → “What do we need after checking usable stock and timely supply?”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Using suitable existing materials may reduce avoidable purchases and waste.

But a damaged kit or stock promised to another group cannot simply be counted as available.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**“In stock” does not always mean “available to use.”**

Condition, location, reservations and timing all matter.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Use the example or small fictional quantities. Reply:

- **Demand and deadline:** ___ units by ___
- **Usable, unallocated stock:** ___
- **Confirmed incoming supply due in time:** ___
- **Remaining projected gap:** ___
- **One assumption I would check:** ___

For this simple case, the gap is the amount by which demand exceeds usable stock plus timely incoming supply. If supply exceeds demand, describe the excess separately.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

The Day 1 need now has a quantity requirement. Your availability calculation is one thing you can later test.

🎉 **Today's win:** You can distinguish demand, available stock and expected supply.

## Day 4 — Plan Backward from the Need Date

🕒 **DAY 4 OF 7 — DELIVERY IS NOT THE FINISH LINE**

You understand the quantity. Today, connect it to time.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. No project-planning software required.**

### 💭 Would a delivery at 9:55 a.m. be enough?

The workshop starts at 10 a.m.

The cartons still need to be received, counted, checked and taken to the room.

Arrival time is not the same as ready-for-use time.

**Lead time** is the elapsed time between agreed start and end points in an activity. Always say which points you mean.

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** A planner works backward from a required delivery date.

**Today:** Include receiving, checking, preparation and handoffs in the schedule.

**Imagine 2050:** AI updates estimated arrival times. People still check capacity, uncertainty and the consequences of a delay.

The question: **“When must each step finish so the next one can work?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“It should arrive before the event.” → “It must arrive early enough to be checked and made ready.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

A last-minute plan can shift pressure to delivery staff and volunteers.

Reasonable preparation time supports quality and more predictable work.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**An earlier order is not always a complete answer.**

If nobody can receive the delivery or store it appropriately, early arrival creates a different problem.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Use this fictional plan or create your own:

| Milestone | Example |
|---|---|
| Learners need the kits | Friday, 10 a.m. |
| Complete kits ready and staged | Thursday, 5 p.m. |
| Incoming kits received for checking | Thursday, 4 p.m. |
| Order accepted by supplier | Tuesday, 4 p.m. |

Assume a confirmed 48-hour order-acceptance-to-receipt lead time, a one-hour checking/preparation slot, available staff and suitable storage. These are exercise assumptions, not guaranteed service levels.

Reply:

- **Needed for use by:** ___
- **Ready and staged by:** ___
- **Received for checking by:** ___
- **Supply lead time and what it covers:** ___
- **Who owns the final readiness check:** ___
- **One timing assumption to verify:** ___

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Write one requirement: **“The materials must be ___ by ___ so that ___.”**

Example: “Twenty complete kits must be checked and staged by Thursday at 5 p.m. for Friday's workshop.”

🎉 **Today's win:** You have a timing plan that includes preparation, not just transport.

## Day 5 — What If the Plan Changes?

🌦️ **DAY 5 OF 7 — BUILD A SMALL RESILIENCE PLAN**

You have quantities and timing. Today, introduce one disruption.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. One scenario is enough.**

### 💭 Four more learners register

Demand rises from twenty kits to twenty-four.

Your usable stock plus confirmed incoming supply still totals twenty.

**The new projected gap is four kits.**

Or imagine the original fourteen incoming kits will arrive after the workshop. The count on the order has not changed, but timely availability has.

**Resilience means preparing to respond and recover when circumstances change.**

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** A planner calls a partner when a shipment is delayed.

**Today:** Teams compare alternative supply, adjusted timing or an agreed change in the plan.

**Imagine 2050:** AI proposes responses sooner. People still judge suitability, fairness, cost and authority to act.

The question: **“What could we change without losing the purpose?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“The plan is fixed.” → “We know what would trigger a review.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

An emergency response may increase cost, workload or transport.

Do not assume a last-minute fix has no consequences for suppliers, staff or the environment.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**More stock is not always the best protection.**

For reusable workshop materials, an agreed borrowing arrangement or an alternative activity may be more suitable than holding a large permanent reserve.

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Choose one disruption:

- Demand increases to twenty-four.
- Fourteen incoming kits arrive too late.
- Two of the six existing kits fail a condition check.

Reply:

- **My disruption:** ___
- **Effect on usable quantity or timing:** ___
- **One possible response:** ___
- **Who would need to agree or act:** ___
- **Trade-off or new risk:** ___
- **My review trigger:** “If ___, we will review ___.”

Possible responses include borrowing suitable kits, finding an approved local alternative or agreeing a revised activity. Verify feasibility rather than treating a backup as guaranteed.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Does the response still meet the original need? If the quantity, item specification or timing changes, update the connected requirements and checks.

🎉 **Today's win:** You have a disruption scenario, a response idea and a review trigger.

## Day 6 — Check What Actually Arrived

🧪 **DAY 6 OF 7 — FROM DELIVERY MESSAGE TO READY MATERIALS**

You planned the flow. Today, check the physical result and its information.

⏱️ **10–15 minutes. Use a fictional list or items you already have.**

### 💭 The message says “Delivered”

Does that mean twenty usable kits are ready?

Not necessarily.

The delivery may contain the wrong item, too few units or materials that fail the agreed condition check.

**Receiving confirms what arrived. Acceptance checks whether it meets agreed requirements. Readiness checks whether it can be used as intended.**

### 🕰️ A thinking journey: 1970 → 2050

Illustrative scenes:

**Picture 1970:** Someone counts goods against a delivery note.

**Today:** Compare the item, quantity, condition, location and status across the physical flow and records.

**Imagine 2050:** Sensors and AI help flag mismatches. People still investigate and decide the appropriate response.

The question: **“Does the evidence match the promise?”**

### 🧠 Mindset shift

**“The delivery is complete.” → “The required materials are checked and ready to use.”**

### 🌍 People and impact

Distributing incomplete kits may leave some learners unable to participate.

Identifying a problem early creates more time to respond fairly.

### ✨ A fresh angle

**A scan or status update is evidence of an event, not proof of every requirement.**

“Received” does not automatically mean “correct, complete and usable.”

### ✍️ Your 7-minute action

Check one requirement using a fictional scenario or a small mock-up.

For the kit example, you could verify:

- Count of complete kits.
- Notebook, pen and correct sheet in a sample.
- Working pen and usable materials.
- Correct room and readiness time.

A sample check only provides evidence about what you sampled; it does not prove every kit is correct.

Reply:

- **Requirement I am checking:** ___
- **Scenario or sample:** ___
- **Expected result:** ___
- **What I checked or observed:** ___
- **Result:** Met / Not met / Not tested yet
- **One correction or readiness action:** ___

If you only plan a check, write **“Not tested yet.”** Do not invent a result.

### 🧵 Follow the thread

Link the check to a Day 4 requirement and your original need. Before actual use, owners would review unresolved shortages, condition issues, location, responsibilities and backup arrangements.

### 🧵 Meet the traceability matrix

A **requirements traceability matrix** connects:

**Origin Story → need → requirements → decisions → tests → readiness → outcomes.**

It helps us ask:

- During requirement gathering: “What must be available, for whom, where and when?”
- During testing: “Which requirement does this check verify?”
- Before go-live or use: “What is ready, what is unresolved and who owns the decision?”
- After use: “Did the intended people have what they needed?”

This is related to, but different from, **product traceability**, which follows items or batches through their supply history and movement.

We will explore both in more depth later. Today, keep the purpose connected to the evidence.

## Coach-only appendix — From Supply Chain Origin Story to traceability

### Keep the challenge proportionate

Daily text submissions provide the starting evidence. Do not require a formal enterprise matrix from beginners.

Later bootcamps can add stable IDs, item definitions, units of measure, source records, owners, versions, dependencies, evidence links, defects and approvals.

The Origin Story gives purpose. It does not replace validated demand, appropriate quality specifications, agreed service conditions or responsible operational governance.

### Shared case and quantity assumptions

- Demand is twenty complete kits, one per learner.
- Each kit contains one usable notebook, one working pen and the correct activity sheet.
- Six complete kits are physically available, usable, at the relevant location and unallocated.
- Fourteen additional complete kits are confirmed incoming and due before the receiving/preparation deadline.
- No competing demand, loss, item substitution, unit conversion or minimum order constraint is included.
- Baseline projected supply is 6 + 14 = 20. Baseline remaining projected gap is zero.
- If demand alone rises to twenty-four, the projected gap becomes four.
- If the fourteen incoming kits arrive too late, only the six existing kits count as timely supply; the projected gap against demand twenty is fourteen.
- If two of the six existing kits are unusable, usable stock falls to four. With fourteen timely incoming kits, projected supply is eighteen and the gap is two.
- Day 5 scenarios are separate changes to the baseline, not cumulative disruptions.
- Expected receipts are not physically received stock. A confirmation is not a guarantee.
- Complete-kit quantities are used to keep the exercise simple. A later lesson could calculate component availability and assembly capacity.

### Shared timing assumptions

- Order accepted Tuesday at 4 p.m.
- Confirmed order-acceptance-to-receipt lead time: 48 elapsed hours.
- Receipt for checking Thursday at 4 p.m.
- One hour allocated for receiving, checking and staging.
- Complete kits ready Thursday at 5 p.m.
- Workshop begins Friday at 10 a.m.
- Relevant staff, capacity and storage are assumed available; no extra calendar, transport or operational restrictions are introduced.
- The 17 hours between Thursday 5 p.m. and Friday 10 a.m. are calendar time, not automatically 17 available working hours.
- These are fictional planning assumptions, not standard lead times.

### Fictional requirements traceability example

**OS-01 — Origin Story:** Learners should be able to participate in the workshop because the required materials are usable and ready.

**NEED-01 — Need to validate:** Twenty learners have complete kits in the correct room before the agreed workshop start.

All records below are proposed and unverified. No real procurement, delivery, test, readiness approval or outcome has occurred.

| Origin / need | Requirement | Proposed decision / evidence | Acceptance check | Readiness consideration | Outcome evidence after use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-01: Twenty complete kits are available for this workshop | DEC-01: Demand record, usable stock count and confirmed incoming quantity | TEST-01: Baseline calculation 6 + 14 = 20; later count actual complete kits and reservations | READY-01: Shortages resolved or an appropriate alternative agreed by the responsible owner | OUT-01: Record whether every participant could access a complete kit |
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-02: Kits are checked and staged in the correct room by Thursday 5 p.m. | DEC-02: Backward plan, delivery location and receiving owner | TEST-02: Rehearse the handoff; verify actual receipt, check and staging evidence against deadlines | READY-02: Receiver, checking capacity, access and storage confirmed | OUT-02: Review actual availability before the session and causes of delay |
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-03: Kits contain the agreed notebook, working pen and correct activity sheet | DEC-03: Item definition and agreed inspection method | TEST-03: Compare contents and condition with agreed criteria; state inspection coverage | READY-03: Nonconforming kits identified and resolved; correct activity-sheet version confirmed | OUT-03: Review material-related interruptions and feedback |
| OS-01 / NEED-01 | REQ-04: A demand increase or delay has a named review owner and feasible response route | DEC-04: Review trigger, escalation contact and assessed alternatives | TEST-04: Rehearse demand twenty-four; identify gap four and route it to the owner | READY-04: Backup feasibility, authority and communication reviewed | OUT-04: Record how changes were managed and their consequences |

Actual implementation needs more detailed requirements, controls and tests appropriate to the materials, risks and operating environment. The example is not exhaustive.

### Two meanings of traceability

| Meaning | What it connects | Beginner example |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements traceability | Purpose, need, requirement, decision, test, readiness and outcome | NEED-01 links to a quantity requirement and the check of complete kits |
| Product or material traceability | Identified items or batches with their relevant source, processing, movement and destination records | A labelled shipment links to its receipt and distribution records |

They can support each other, but they are not interchangeable.

A requirements matrix does not establish item provenance. A barcode scan does not prove requirements were tested. The evidence, identifiers and controls must fit the purpose.

In regulated or safety-critical contexts, additional traceability requirements need specialist analysis. The workshop example is intentionally simple and non-regulated.

### What the requirements matrix should reveal

- A requirement with no agreed need.
- A calculation using unavailable, reserved or late stock as though it were usable.
- A test without a relevant requirement, or a requirement without a test.
- A changed demand quantity that has not flowed through supply and readiness checks.
- A delivery status treated as proof of item quality.
- A local task marked complete while the final need remains unmet.
- A plan presented as an observed outcome.

Links can be many-to-many and must work in both directions. An ID is a reference, not proof. Keep actual evidence, status, owner and revision history.

Go-live readiness also involves people, capacity, information, exception handling, support and appropriate approvals. A complete matrix alone is not a readiness decision.

### A small outcome-measure plan

| Intended benefit | Possible evidence | Interpretation question |
|---|---|---|
| Learners have the right materials on time | Kit count, condition and actual ready-for-use time | Were kits complete and at the point of use? |
| Handoffs are dependable | Acknowledged receiving/staging records | Did the next owner receive both materials and necessary information? |
| Unnecessary waste is reduced | Leftover usable items and material disposal records | Was lower waste achieved without leaving people short? |
| Disruptions are handled responsibly | Review log, response actions and feedback | Was the backup feasible, and who carried the extra effort or cost? |

Agree definitions, baselines, periods and owners before real evaluation. Do not invent targets or claim a result from a single unverified status.

### Connection to the other Origin Story challenges

The shared pattern is:

**Purpose → need → requirements → decision → checks → readiness → outcome.**

- Procurement follows a buying decision and its consequences.
- HR follows an employee-experience improvement.
- CX follows a customer goal and service promise.
- Finance follows resources, cash timing and a decision.
- SCM connects demand, materials, information and coordinated delivery.

Use the same habits in Business Transformation Management and Leadership & Identity Transformation, adapting evidence to the context.

### Facilitation notes

- Reward connected reasoning rather than elaborate submissions.
- On Day 2, ask who owns the next handoff.
- On Day 3, ask whether stock is usable, unallocated and timely.
- On Day 4, distinguish arrival from ready-for-use time.
- On Day 5, accept one scenario; do not require a full risk register.
- On Day 6, accept “Not tested yet” with a useful plan.
- On Day 7, distinguish local measures from the end-to-end outcome.
- Do not promise employment, certification, savings, guaranteed delivery or automatic programme inclusion.
